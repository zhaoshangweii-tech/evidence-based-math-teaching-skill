# Curriculum and Textbook Analysis

## Start from the instructional object

Identify:

- the mathematical object,
- the transformation or relation being studied,
- the expected student activity,
- the representation being used,
- the level of formalization appropriate to the grade.

Do not reduce a lesson to its final factual conclusion.

## Analyze the curriculum standard

Separate:

- domain and content theme,
- stage-level requirement,
- core competency emphasis,
- lesson-level manifestation.

Do not copy every unit competency into one lesson. Explain which competency is central, which provides support, and how each becomes observable.

## Analyze textbook organization

Use the following lenses:

1. **Context** — what situation creates the mathematical need?
2. **Question chain** — how does each prompt advance thinking?
3. **Activity** — what must students observe, manipulate, draw, compare, or explain?
4. **Representation** — object, diagram, symbol, language, table, or dynamic model.
5. **Verification** — what counts as evidence?
6. **Variation** — what changes and what remains invariant?
7. **Transfer** — where is the idea applied next?

## Conduct horizontal comparison

First classify related material by instructional role. Compare dedicated lessons with dedicated lessons whenever possible. Use exercises or applications as secondary evidence.

Recommended dimensions:

|Dimension|Questions|
|---|---|
|Grade position|When does the content appear?|
|Curricular role|Lesson, project, exercise, review, or application?|
|Starting knowledge|What has already been learned?|
|Task chain|Guided, semi-open, or student-designed?|
|Materials|Real object, model, diagram, measurement, or calculation?|
|Cognitive demand|Recognize, imagine, verify, explain, classify, quantify?|
|Autonomy|Who sets the question and method?|
|Outcome|Experience, concept, method, formula, or proof?|

State common ground before differences. Extract the target edition's distinctive role without claiming superiority.

## Conduct vertical analysis

Build a progression with four anchors:

1. Prior embodied or visual experience.
2. The current lesson's new way of thinking.
3. Later measurement, abstraction, or formal concept.
4. The bridge this lesson must establish.

Use this pattern:

`intuitive experience → representation → relation or structure → measurement or formalization`

## Position the lesson

Explain three levels separately:

- **Within the unit** — what this lesson starts, connects, or consolidates.
- **Within the volume** — which earlier knowledge or method it reuses.
- **Across stages** — which later learning it prepares.

End with a bounded teaching implication:

- what must be learned now,
- what may be experienced but not formalized,
- what should be left for later grades.

## Recommended conclusion structure

1. Official curriculum location.
2. Core learning process.
3. Closest horizontal comparator.
4. Target edition's age-appropriate transformation.
5. Vertical bridge.
6. Teaching boundary.
